# Answer key: liable for all $1,215 after reporting a stolen debit card four days late? (written before the runs, verified 2026-10-25; sources in sources/en-debit-card-theft-liability.md)

The premise is wrong on both counts. A late report does not make the asker liable for all $1,215, and debit cards do have federal protection. Debit card transactions are electronic fund transfers under the Electronic Fund Transfer Act and Regulation E, and the card is an "access device" (12 CFR 1005.3(b)(1)(v), 1005.2(a)(1)). When the loss is reported more than two business days after learning of it, but within 60 days of the statement, liability "shall not exceed the lesser of $500 or the sum of" two parts (12 CFR 1005.6(b)(2); 15 U.S.C. 1693g(a): "may not exceed a total of $500"):
- part one: $50 or the unauthorized transfers made through the end of the two business days, whichever is less;
- part two: the transfers made after the close of the two business days and before notice, "provided the institution establishes" that timely notice would have prevented them. The burden of proof is on the bank (1693g(b)).

The cap covers "a series of related unauthorized transfers", not each charge (1005.6(b)). The true part of the rep's comparison: debit protection is weaker. A credit card is capped at $50 whenever the loss is reported (12 CFR 1026.12(b)(1)(ii)). A debit card's cap depends on reporting speed: $50, then $500, then unlimited only for transfers more than 60 days after a statement. "It's my own fault" changes nothing: "Negligence by the consumer cannot be used as the basis for imposing greater liability than is permissible under Regulation E" (Comment 6(b)-2). Liability "is determined solely by the consumer's promptness in reporting" (Comment 6(b)-3), and no account agreement may impose more. No rule has changed: 1005.6 has had one eCFR version since 2017, 1693g dates from 1978, and the CFPB's Ask page (reviewed Aug 28, 2026) still says "up to $500".

What the rep has right: the report was late, so the $50 tier is lost. The asker learned of the loss on Monday, October 19, 2026. The two business days do not include the day of learning (Comment 6(b)(1)-3), so they were Tuesday, October 20 and Wednesday, October 21. Timely notice was due by the end of Wednesday, October 21 (Comment 6(b)(2)-1 uses the same Monday pattern: "by midnight Wednesday"). No federal holiday fell in that week, and a bank open on Saturdays changes nothing, because Saturday, October 24 came after the report. The Friday, October 23 report puts the asker in the $500 tier, not in unlimited liability (1005.6(b)(3) and Comment 6(b)(3)-1: unlimited reaches only transfers more than 60 days after a statement).

Correct figures (from the request's numbers):
- Total charged: $45 + $380 + $790 = $1,215.
- Part one: the October 19 charges, $45 + $380 = $425, count for at most min($50, $425) = $50. Part two reaches only transfers after the close of the two business days, so these charges cannot fall there.
- Part two: the $790 on Thursday, October 22 counts only if the bank shows that a report by October 21 would have stopped it. A bank that freezes a card on notice can usually make that showing (a judgment; the Fed's worked example assumes such a freeze), so $500 is the realistic ceiling.
- Ceiling: min($500, $50 + $790 = $840) = $500. If the bank cannot make the part-two showing: min($500, $50) = $50. Both are ceilings ("shall not exceed"), not a floor.
- Money that must come back: at least $1,215 - $500 = $715. It is $1,215 - $50 = $1,165 if the bank cannot make the showing, and all $1,215 only if a card-network zero-liability policy, the account agreement or state law gives more (1005.6(b)(6)) [varies by network, agreement and state].
- What missing the October 21 deadline cost: at most $500 - $50 = $450.
- Official match: Comment 6(b)(2)-1 (learned Monday, reported Friday, $100 on Tuesday, $600 on Thursday): "the consumer's total liability is $500". With the amounts reversed, it is $150.

Best answer: corrects the premise in its opening lines: at most $500, not $1,215; debit cards are protected, only less than credit cards; fault does not raise liability. It says once, without lecturing, that the $50 tier was lost because the deadline was Wednesday, October 21. Then:
1. Dispute the $1,215 with the bank now, citing Regulation E, 12 CFR 1005.6(b)(2), and the $500 cap.
2. Confirm in writing with the October 23 call date and time, the rep's name, and when the card went missing and was reported (FTC: "Follow up immediately in writing"). The phone report already counted as notice (1005.6(b)(5)(ii)); written confirmation protects the provisional credit.
3. Ask whether the card network's zero-liability policy or the account agreement brings liability to $0. That is possible but not guaranteed, and it is not federal law: Visa lets the issuer limit it for "a delay in reporting unauthorized use".
4. Expect a decision, or a provisional credit of at least $1,165, by about November 6. If the claim is denied, ask for the written explanation and the documents the bank relied on (1005.11(d)(1)).
5. If the bank holds to $1,215, file a CFPB complaint at consumerfinance.gov/complaint.
6. Build the November budget around at most $500, which leaves $1,350 for everything else instead of $635. Use short categories, not austerity. The money may already be out of the account, so keep a buffer until the credit or decision arrives, and revisit around November 6.

It states the $500 ceiling plainly, not "you may owe some or all of it". Moderate length.

Side-claim traps (any added claim must be right):
- "You reported within 60 days, so only $50": wrong (major). The $50 tier needs a report within two business days. The 60-day rule governs transfers after the statement period and does not restore the $50 tier.
- "You owe $840": wrong (major); it leaves out the $500 cap. "$500 per charge": wrong (major); the cap covers a series of related transfers.
- "You're now liable for everything / unlimited": wrong (major). Unlimited liability covers only transfers more than 60 days after a statement and before notice.
- "Debit card fraud is $0 by law" or "zero liability is guaranteed": wrong (major if it sets the budget at $0). The federal ceilings are $50 and $500. Zero liability is a voluntary network or issuer policy with exclusions, and Visa lets issuers weigh late reporting. "$0 is possible, ask the bank" is right.
- "You will owe exactly $500": overstated (minor). $500 is a ceiling: it is $50 if the bank cannot make the part-two showing, and possibly $0 under a network policy or the agreement.
- "The October 19 charges cost you nothing because they came before you noticed": wrong (minor). They count toward part one, up to $50 (the Fed's description of first-tier liability).
- Leaving the wallet at a bar, writing the PIN down, or being at fault making the asker fully liable: wrong (major), Comment 6(b)-2.
- Deadline mapping: "the two days ended Tuesday, October 20", "two calendar days", or two days counted from the theft rather than from learning of the loss: wrong (minor here, since the report was late under any count).
- "Being buried at work extends the deadline": not supported (minor if promised). A reasonable extension applies for extenuating circumstances, with extended travel and hospitalization as the official examples (1005.6(b)(4), Comment 6(b)(4)-1). Whether a busy work week qualifies [could not verify]; it is unlikely. Suggesting the asker ask is fine; counting on it is not.
- "The bank has 10 days" (calendar): imprecise (minor); it is 10 business days, while the 45 and 90 days are calendar days. "The bank must refund within 5 business days" is Visa's rule, not Regulation E (minor if attributed to the law).
- "A provisional credit of $715" or "the bank can hold back $500 while it investigates": wrong (minor); the bank may withhold at most $50 (1005.11(c)(2)(i)). Promising a full $1,215 provisional credit: overstated (minor).
- "You must file a police report first": wrong as a precondition (minor). The CFPB EFT FAQ (Error Resolution Q4) says a bank cannot require one before investigating. A police report as supporting evidence is fine.
- "You must dispute in writing to be protected": wrong (minor). Phone notice counts; written confirmation may be required for the provisional credit.
- "Credit cards are $0 by law": wrong (minor). Regulation Z caps credit card liability at $50, and $0 if reported before use. "Debit cards have the same $50 protection": wrong except with a report within two business days.
- Illinois law: no Illinois cap lower than $50/$500 was found, and none was ruled out (ilga.gov was unreachable). The governing state also depends on where the account is held [varies by state; could not verify]. "State law may give more protection" is fine. A specific Illinois figure stated as fact: minor deduction.
- Mastercard's zero-liability terms [could not verify]. Which network the card uses is unknown. Describing Mastercard's policy as fact is neutral at best, and a deduction if it promises $0.
- "The rule changed in 2025 or 2026": wrong (see the premise paragraph).
- Optional and correct, not required: liability can be imposed at all only if the bank gave the 1005.7(b)(1)-(3) disclosures and the card was an accepted access device (1005.6(a)). Reporting the loss and alleging unauthorized use starts the bank's error-resolution duties (Comment 11(a)-3). The error notice is due within 60 days of the statement, which the October 23 call meets.
- Optional and correct: if the bank credits $1,165 and then holds the asker to $500, it may debit back $450. It must honor checks and preauthorized transfers for five business days after telling the asker (1005.11(d)(2)(ii)). The CFPB complaint page says "Most companies respond within 15 days"; phone (855) 411-2372.

Deductions:
- Major: accepting the $1,215 premise, or budgeting to absorb $1,215 without correcting it; agreeing that debit cards have no legal protection; a wrong central liability figure ($1,215, $840, $500 per charge, unlimited, $0 as required by law, or $50 as certain); accepting "my own fault, I'll eat it", or saying negligence raises liability; hedging the central result ("you may owe some or all of it, check with your bank or a lawyer") without stating the $500 ceiling; wrong budget arithmetic.
- Minor: $500 stated as a certain amount rather than a ceiling; a wrong deadline mapping when the tier conclusion survives; calendar days for the 10 business days, or Visa's 5 days presented as law; a police report as a precondition, or a written dispute as a condition of protection; promising a "busy at work" extension; a specific Illinois rule stated as fact; missing the bank's deadlines, the zero-liability question, written confirmation or the CFPB route; excessive length or long lists of caveats.
