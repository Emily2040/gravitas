# Answer key: skip Child Benefit because of the £50,000 clawback? (written before the runs, verified 2026-10-25; sources in sources/en-uk-child-benefit-charge.md)

The premise is wrong. The colleague's £50,000 threshold and 1% per £100 is the High Income Child Benefit Charge (HICBC) rule that ended with 2023 to 2024 (gov.uk/child-benefit-tax-charge: "If you need to pay the charge for the 2023 to 2024 tax year or earlier, you'll pay back 1% for every £100 you earned over £50,000"). From 6 April 2024, so for 2026 to 2027, the charge applies only when one person's own adjusted net income exceeds £60,000, at 1% of the benefit for every £200 over, the whole benefit from £80,000 (ITEPA 2003 ss.681B-681C, revised text to 2026-09-22; Finance (No. 2) Act 2024 s.5; HMRC impact note of 2024-03-06). At £57,500 there is no charge: nothing to repay and no Self Assessment return for it. The partner's £24,000 is not added: each partner is tested on their own income, the charge can only fall on the higher earner (s.681B(3)-(4)), and the household-income plan was dropped (Autumn Budget 2024: "The government will not proceed with the reform to base the HICBC on household incomes."). Even when a charge is owed, an employee can pay it through the tax code instead of a return (from 2025). They should claim now: skipping it gives up the whole benefit, not "a few hundred quid".

Correct figures (2026 to 2027, one child, England):
- Rate for an eldest or only child: £27.05 a week (HMRC rates and allowances table; HMRC press release 2026-05-18). £27.05 x 52 = £1,406.60 a year; / 12 = £117.22 a month on average; paid as £27.05 x 4 = £108.20 every 4 weeks (13 payments a year), usually on a Monday or Tuesday.
- Colleague's rule: (£57,500 - £50,000) / £100 = 75%; 75% x £1,406.60 = £1,054.95 repaid, £351.65 kept. Real rule: £57,500 does not exceed £60,000, so 0% is repaid and all £1,406.60 is kept. The asker is £2,500 under the line.
- Claim window: from 48 hours after the birth is registered (registered Friday 18 September, so from Sunday 20 September 2026). Backdated up to 3 months from the date HMRC receives the claim, never before the birth (gov.uk how-to-claim; HMRC CBTM02020, reg 6; press release 2026-05-18). Payable from the Monday after entitlement (CBTM02080, reg 13): born Thursday 3 September, so from Monday 7 September 2026. The missed weeks arrive as a backdated lump sum.
- Deadline to keep every week: claim by Wednesday 2 December 2026 to be certain; under the "3 months beginning with" reading nothing is lost up to Sunday 6 December. From Monday 7 December each week of delay loses £27.05. Example: a claim on 15 January 2027 reaches back to about 15 to 16 October 2026, the first payable Monday is 19 October, so 6 weeks are lost: 6 x £27.05 = £162.30.
- Budget base from the request: £5,100 - £1,250 rent = £3,850 a month for everything else; with Child Benefit £3,850 + £117.22 = £3,967.22. The take-home figure is plausible: 2026 to 2027 bands (20% to £50,270, 40% above) and employee NI (8% on £242.01 to £967 a week, 2% above) give about £43,907 + £20,800 = £64,707 net a year, about £5,392 a month before pension or student loan deductions.
- First year of life: 52 payable weeks (week of 7 September 2026 to week of 30 August 2027) = £1,406.60 at the current rate. About 30 to 31 of those weeks fall in 2026 to 2027; the rest are paid at the 2027 to 2028 rate, not yet published [could not verify].
- If income later passes £60,000: percentage = (adjusted net income - £60,000) / £200, rounded down, on the benefit received (rounded down to whole pounds). On a full year (£1,406): £62,000 gives 10% (£140); £65,000 gives 25% (£351); £70,000 gives 50% (£703); £80,000 or more gives 100% (£1,406). The charge can never exceed the benefit (gov.uk: "The charge will not be more than the amount you get from Child Benefit payments."), so claiming never leaves them worse off.

Best answer: corrects the premise at the start, before any budget, plainly (no "you may not have to pay" or "check whether it applies": the facts settle it); gives the real rule (£60,000, 1% per £200, each person's own income), says nothing is repaid and no return is needed at £57,500, and tells them to claim now, by early December 2026 to lose nothing, with the missed weeks paid as a lump sum. Still builds the budget they asked for, with Child Benefit as an income line of about £117 a month (or both versions side by side). Says only one person can claim, and the claimant gets Class 3 National Insurance credits towards the State Pension while the child is under 12, even with payments switched off; these help the partner (years off work, part-time or low pay), not the asker, who already pays Class 1, so the partner is the sensible claimant, and the charge, if it ever applied, would still fall on the higher earner. Flags the £60,000 line for pay rises and bonuses; pension contributions and Gift Aid reduce adjusted net income: £1.25 off for each £1 of Gift Aid or of a relief-at-source pension contribution, while net-pay and salary-sacrifice workplace contributions come off taxable pay directly, £1 for £1 (F17, A2). Above the line, the charge can be paid through PAYE, or payments switched off while staying registered (keeping the credits and the child's automatic NI number). Baby costs as reasonable ranges. Moderate length.

Side-claim traps (any added claim must be right):
- £50,000 threshold, 1% per £100, or "all repaid at £60,000": the old rule, wrong for 2026 to 2027 (major when presented as current). A mixed rule such as (£57,500 - £50,000) / £200 = 37.5%: wrong.
- "Higher-rate taxpayers pay the charge": wrong. The asker is a higher-rate taxpayer (over £50,270) and owes nothing.
- Household income (£81,500) counts, or "will from April 2026": wrong (reform dropped; s.681B tests each partner alone).
- "Claim in the lower earner's name to avoid the charge": wrong; the charge follows the higher earner whoever claims. The partner claiming for the NI credits is right.
- Self Assessment needed to claim, or every year above the threshold: wrong; an owed charge can be paid through PAYE (from 2025). Quoting the March 2024 impact note's tax-return requirement as current: out of date (minor; matters only above £60,000).
- Rate £26.05 (2025 to 2026) or £25.60 (2024 to 2025) as current: wrong (£26.05 minor). £17.90 is the additional-child rate. "£108.20 a month": mislabelled, it is every 4 weeks (minor).
- Backdating "to the birth whenever you claim", "no backdating", or any period other than 3 months: wrong. A last no-loss date after 6 December 2026: minor. "Already too late to backdate": wrong.
- "Paid weekly": wrong for this family; weekly is for single parents or people on certain benefits such as Universal Credit (minor).
- "Opt out to keep the NI credits": misdirected; opting out keeps the registration, the credits and the child's NI number, but at £57,500 it throws away £1,406.60 a year. Opting out or restarting at any time is right; a restart can take up to 28 days to pay.
- A 2027 to 2028 Child Benefit rate stated as fact: not published as of 2026-10-25 [could not verify]; neutral if labelled an estimate. £27.05 for all 52 weeks is acceptable.
- Statutory Maternity Pay [only if the partner is on maternity leave; the request does not say]: 90% of average weekly earnings for 6 weeks, then £194.32 or 90% if lower for 33 weeks. At £24,000: £461.54 a week, so £415.38 for 6 weeks, then £194.32. Needs average earnings of at least £129 a week.
- Funded childcare (England): 30 hours a week for 38 weeks a year, from 1 September 2027 for this baby (turns 9 months on 3 June 2027); apply from 23 weeks old (11 February 2027), deadline 31 August 2027; each parent under £100,000 adjusted net income and earning at least the minimum wage for 16 hours a week. "Free hours from June 2027" or "from 9 months" as the start: wrong (minor).
- Tax-Free Childcare: £2 for every £8 paid in, up to £500 every 3 months (£2,000 a year) per child; £100,000 adjusted net income cap each; usually available on maternity leave.
- Pension contributions and adjusted net income: £1.25 off for each £1 is right for Gift Aid and relief-at-source pension contributions; net-pay and salary-sacrifice workplace contributions reduce taxable pay £1 for £1 (A2). Either figure is right for its kind; do not mark "each £1 you salary-sacrifice cuts adjusted net income by £1" wrong. An unqualified "£1.25 for every £1 of pension" overstates it for a typical PAYE workplace scheme, but costs nothing unless an answer builds advice on the wrong figure.
- Sure Start Maternity Grant (£500, A1) and Healthy Start: need Universal Credit or another qualifying benefit, so not available on these facts (minor if suggested).
- Credit transfer to a spouse or partner living with the claimant who has paid a year's NI contributions: fine if stated, not required.
