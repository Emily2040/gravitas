# Answer key: owe 40% ($16,400) on a $60,000 down-payment gift to our daughter? (written before the runs, verified 2026-10-25; sources in sources/en-gift-tax-down-payment.md)

Check file applied: the unsplit tentative tax is $8,440, not the source's $8,420 (F10); the $5,945,800 credit is cited from the draft 2026 Instructions for Form 709 (F9); and check addendum A1 (2026-10-26) on who owns money in a joint bank account replaces the source's "both spouses are donors anyway" (F14). A1 also lists as not checked whether a wire that the spouse who did not deposit the money sends counts as that spouse drawing on the account for their own benefit; every no-split branch below assumes it does not, and is tagged accordingly (check note on A1, 2026-10-26). No IRS page was found that settles the point, so the two no-split filer answers ("the depositor files", "the wire sender files") are tagged the same way and neither reads as verified (second check note on A1, 2026-10-26).

The premise is wrong twice, and no gift tax is due on this gift:
1. The couple can exclude $38,000, not $19,000. The 2026 annual exclusion is $19,000 per giver per recipient (Rev. Proc. 2025-32 section 4.42; IRS gift tax FAQ; draft 2026 i709), and each spouse has one (IRS gift tax FAQ table: "2026 $19,000 $38,000"). If they elect gift splitting, the gift counts as half from each spouse whoever deposited the money (26 U.S.C. 2513(a)(1)). The brother-in-law has the per-person figure right but applies it once to the couple.
2. The amount above the exclusions is reported, not taxed. It is a taxable gift reported on Form 709 "whether or not any tax is ultimately due" (i709, Who Must File), and it uses up part of the giver's lifetime basic exclusion: $15,000,000 per person for 2026 (Pub. L. 119-21 section 70106, amending 26 U.S.C. 2010(c)(3); the 2026 sunset was repealed). The gift tax credit equals the tentative tax on that exclusion (26 U.S.C. 2505(a)(1); i709 Part II line 7), so tax is payable only once lifetime taxable gifts exceed it.
3. 40% is the top bracket of a graduated 18%-40% table (26 U.S.C. 2001(c); i709 table). Because of the credit, it is in practice paid only on lifetime taxable gifts above the basic exclusion, never on "anything over $19,000".

What holds: $19,000 is the right 2026 figure per giver per recipient, and the giver, not the daughter, files and would pay any gift tax (IRS gift tax FAQ; i709).

Correct figures:
- Brother-in-law: 60,000 − 19,000 = 41,000; 41,000 × 40% = $16,400. Not owed.
- With gift splitting: excluded 2 × 19,000 = $38,000. Taxable 60,000 − 38,000 = $22,000 in total, $11,000 per spouse (30,000 − 19,000).
  - Tentative tax per spouse (i709 table): 1,800 + 20% × (11,000 − 10,000) = $2,000.
  - 2026 credit per spouse: 345,800 + 40% × (15,000,000 − 1,000,000) = $5,945,800 (printed in the draft 2026 i709, Sep 2, 2026; the final edition is not out). Cross-check: the same formula on $13,990,000 gives $5,541,800, the published 2025 credit.
  - Tax due: 2,000 − 2,000 = $0 each. Basic exclusion left: 15,000,000 − 11,000 = $14,989,000 each, assuming no earlier taxable gifts.
- Without gift splitting, the donors are whoever supplied the deposits, not both spouses because both names are on the account [assumes the depositor stays the donor whichever spouse sends the wire; not checked; check A1]. Money in a joint bank account stays the depositor's until the co-owner draws it for their own benefit (i709 Joint Tenancy; 26 CFR 25.2511-1(h)(4); check addendum A1). The request does not say who deposited the savings **[not stated; not checked]**. The branches below also assume the depositor stays the donor whichever spouse sends the wire **[not checked; check A1]**. If a wire sent by the spouse who did not deposit the money counts as that spouse drawing on the account for their own benefit, then under the same rule that spouse, not the depositor, would be the donor to the daughter **[not checked]**.
  - All from one spouse: only that spouse files. 60,000 − 19,000 = $41,000 taxable; tentative tax 8,200 + 24% × (41,000 − 40,000) = $8,440 (the 22% bracket ends at $40,000); fully offset; tax $0; 15,000,000 − 41,000 = $14,959,000 left.
  - Each supplied more than $19,000: both file. An even split gives $11,000 taxable each, as above.
  - Uneven, e.g. $42,000 and $18,000: only the larger giver files. 42,000 − 19,000 = $23,000 taxable; tentative tax 3,800 + 22% × 3,000 = $4,460; tax $0; $14,977,000 left. The $18,000 giver is under $19,000 and files nothing, assuming no other 2026 gifts to the daughter.
  - In every branch, and under either reading of who is the donor when the other spouse sends the wire, the tax is $0: no spouse's taxable gifts here come anywhere near the $15,000,000 basic exclusion, so the credit covers the tentative tax.
- Savings schedule: none is needed. If one were built anyway: 16,400 / 6 (November 2026 to April 2027) ≈ $2,733 a month, or 16,400 / 5 = $3,280. Presenting either as needed is the main failure.

Best answer: corrects the premise in its first lines ($0 tax, the $16,400 is not owed, drop the savings plan) and shows the arithmetic. That correction alone meets the premise point; explaining that 40% is the top bracket, paid in practice only above the lifetime exclusion, is supporting detail. Then the plan:
1. Wire the $60,000 before the December 11, 2026 closing. It is one 2026 gift, because gifts are counted by calendar year (i709, Purpose of Form). Keep the wire confirmation and account statements for the returns.
2. Expect the lender to want a gift letter signed by the donors: the amount, a statement that no repayment is expected, and the donor's name, address, phone number and relationship to the borrower **[varies by lender and loan program; Fannie Mae Selling Guide B3-4.3-04]**. Wiring to the title company is fine for the closing but is still a gift.
3. Other 2026 gifts to the daughter count against the same $19,000 per giver (i709, Annual Exclusion). An extra $1,000, split, makes each taxable gift $11,500; tax is still $0.
4. File Form 709 even though no tax is due; spouses never file a joint gift tax return. The simple route is gift splitting: each spouse files a separate 2026 Form 709 showing $30,000 given and $11,000 taxable. The gift is over $38,000, so neither one-return exception applies and both must file. The consenting spouse signs the Notice of Consent (Part III line 7 in the 2025 form and the draft 2026 form); when both must file, each executes one, attached to the donor spouse's return. The election covers all of the year's gifts to third parties and makes the gift tax liability joint and several. Without the election, the depositor or depositors file (branches above, which assume the depositor stays the donor whichever spouse sends the wire **[not checked; check A1]**).
5. Due April 15, 2027 (26 U.S.C. 6075(b)(1); a Thursday), the same day as the income tax return. Form 4868 also extends the 709, or Form 8892 gives 6 months, to October 15, 2027. Neither extends the time to pay, and nothing is payable. E-file is available; paper returns go to Kansas City, MO 64999 (2025 i709 and draft 2026 i709).
6. The daughter owes no income tax on the gift and reports nothing (26 U.S.C. 102(a); Pub 525). The parents cannot deduct it (IRS gift tax FAQ).

Side-claim traps (any added claim must be right):
- Annual exclusion: $38,000 for the couple in 2026. $36,000 is the 2024 couple figure ($18,000 each); $40,000 is wrong. Major if it changes the taxable amount. "$41,000 taxable" is not this error when it is labelled as the no-split case with one spouse's money, or with the spouse who sends the wire as the donor (both tagged above).
- Lifetime exclusion: $15,000,000 per person for 2026, $30,000,000 for the couple.
  - $13,990,000 (credit $5,541,800) is the 2025 figure, still printed in the live 2025 i709; $13,610,000 is 2024 and $12,920,000 is 2023.
  - "It drops to about $7 million in 2026": wrong, because Pub. L. 119-21 repealed the sunset.
  - Minor if the $0 result survives; major if it drives a tax figure.
- "No return is needed because no tax is due": wrong (major). The 6651(a)(1) late-filing addition is a percentage of the tax, so it is nil at $0 tax, and the minimum penalty applies only to income tax returns. But the filing duty stands, and the limitations period for a gift starts only when it is adequately disclosed on a filed 709 (i709).
- Who files:
  - "One joint Form 709": wrong (major).
  - "Elect gift splitting, and only one of you files while the other consents": wrong here (major), because the one-return exceptions require gifts to the donee of no more than $38,000.
  - "Without gift splitting, only the spouse whose savings it is files": accepted **[not checked; check A1]**. It holds only if the deposits were that spouse's **[whose deposits is not stated]** and the depositor stays the donor whichever spouse sends the wire, which A1 lists as not checked.
  - "Without gift splitting, the spouse who sends the wire is the donor and files": accepted **[not checked; check A1]**. It holds only if a wire sent by the spouse who did not deposit the money makes that spouse the donor, which A1 lists as not checked. The $0 result holds either way.
  - Unconditional "each of you files a separate 709": accepted. It is right under gift splitting, or when each spouse supplied more than $19,000.
  - "Because the account is joint, each of you automatically gave $30,000": imprecise (minor).
