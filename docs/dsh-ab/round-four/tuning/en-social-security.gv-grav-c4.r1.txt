## The $2,400 isn't your age-62 amount

Your full retirement age is 67 (born 1965). Claiming at 62 is roughly 59 months early — you're not 62 for the whole of March, so April 2027 is your earliest month of entitlement, with the first payment in May — and that reduction is permanent. It comes to about **70.4% of your age-67 amount: roughly $1,690 a month, not $2,400**.

Forty years of contributions is why you're insured and can claim at all. It doesn't remove the early-filing reduction — that's set by claiming age, not by how long you paid in.

## Working while you claim triggers the earnings test

Under FRA, SSA withholds $1 of benefits for every $2 you earn above an annual limit. For 2027 the limit is **$25,560** ([SSA's 2027 figures](https://adasoutheast.org/cost-of-living-adjustment-cola-information-for-2027/); [SSA fact sheet](https://www.ssa.gov/news/en/cola/factsheets/2027.html)). At $38,000 you're $12,440 over, so about **$6,220 a year is withheld** — roughly four monthly checks. There's no exemption for long contribution histories.

## Four things that matter for the budget

- **The statement is in today's dollars.** COLAs of 2.8% (2026) and 3.6% (2027) will lift both numbers if your $2,400 is 2025 dollars — roughly 6.5%, so about $1,800/month at 62 and ~$3,550 net. The table above is the conservative version.
- **Checks arrive unevenly.** SSA withholds whole checks, not slices, so expect roughly four months a year with no payment. If your actual earnings exceed their estimate, they recover the difference afterward — don't spend the early checks.
- **The withheld months aren't lost forever.** At 67, SSA recomputes the reduction to credit months when no benefit was payable — your benefit would rise to roughly $1,830. Better, but still short of $2,400.
- **Waiting is worth pricing.** At 67 you'd get the full $2,400 with no earnings test at all. Since you'd forfeit about 30% of an early benefit to the test anyway, the real cost of waiting is much smaller than it looks, and the increase is permanent.
