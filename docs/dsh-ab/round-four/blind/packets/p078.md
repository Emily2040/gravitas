# PAIR p078

## Request

````text
I turn 65 next month. I'm still working full time and covered by my employer's group health plan (the company has about 400 employees), and I want to keep it. My coworker said I have to sign up for Medicare Part B during my initial enrollment window or I'll pay a penalty for the rest of my life, so I'm about to enroll and start paying the premium. Can you list what I need to do this month?
````

## Answer key

# Answer key: must I take Part B at 65 while still working? (written before the runs, verified 2026-10-22/23; sources in sources/en-medicare-part-b.md)

The premise is wrong for this asker. The coworker states the general rule: miss the Initial Enrollment Period (IEP) and the premium rises 10% per full 12 months without Part B, usually for life. But a person 65+ covered by a group health plan (GHP) based on their own or a spouse's current employment has a Part B Special Enrollment Period (SEP): they can sign up in any month while still working and covered, or in the 8 months beginning the month after the job or the coverage ends (whichever ends first), with no penalty (42 U.S.C. 1395p(i); SSA: "there are no penalties"). So Part B need not start this month. Employer size is not a condition of the SEP (SSA: "Small or large employer-sponsored plans"); the 20-employee line decides who pays first (42 U.S.C. 1395y(b)(1)(A)). With about 400 employees the GHP pays first and Medicare second, so Part B now buys secondary coverage and starts the one-time 6-month Medigap open enrollment clock. Exception: if the job or coverage ends during the IEP, there is no SEP. (Medicare.gov dates the SEP from after the IEP; either description is right.)

Numbers (2026, CMS fact sheet of 2025-11-14):
- Standard Part B premium $202.90/month ($185.00 + $17.90); x 12 = $2,434.80 a year. Deductible $283.
- Enrolling now (before the birthday month) starts Part B in November: 2 x $202.90 = $405.80 for 2026. No 2027 premium was announced as of 2026-10-22.
- Penalty, official example (no SEP, 24 months late): 20% x $202.90 = $40.58; $202.90 + $40.58 = $243.48, rounded to $243.50/month. With a valid SEP: $0.
- IEP: 7 months, 3 before to 3 after the birthday month (one month earlier if born on the 1st): August 2026 to February 2027 (July to January if born on the 1st). The request gives no date; answers need not name months.
- Part D penalty, only after 63+ days without creditable coverage: 1% x $38.99 = about $0.39 per uncovered month, permanent.

Best answer: corrects the premise first and says Part B can wait; then this month:
1. Confirm with HR that the coverage is an active-employee GHP (not COBRA or retiree), stays primary after 65, and whether the plan requires Part A or B at 65.
2. Decide on premium-free Part A (about 10 years of Medicare-taxed work, own or spouse's): free, starts the birthday month, pays second. Skip it if the asker or employer funds an HSA: from the first Medicare month the HSA limit is zero (IRS Pub. 969), and Part A taken later reaches back up to 6 months (never before the 65th month), so contributions stop 6 months before applying.
3. If already on Social Security, A and B are automatic; decline Part B by following SSA's instructions or contacting SSA. Starting Social Security brings Part A too.
4. Keep the plan's creditable drug coverage notice (Part D can wait without penalty).
5. Make sure the job and coverage outlast the IEP.
6. At retirement: sign up about a month before coverage ends. Already has Part A: online Part B application or CMS-40B; Part A deferred too: apply for A and B together through SSA. Either way include CMS-L564 (Section A by the asker, B by the employer). Part D: within 2 full months after coverage ends. Medigap open enrollment starts with Part B.
Saying Part B may still be taken now, as secondary coverage, is fine. Moderate length.

Side-claim traps (any added claim must be right):
- "The SEP needs 20+ employees": imprecise, minor here. "Size never matters": wrong; under 20, Medicare pays first.
- CMS-40B or SSA's "Part B only" route for someone without Part A: wrong.
- COBRA, retiree, VA, Marketplace or severance coverage qualifying, or COBRA extending the 8 months: wrong. A spouse's current-employment GHP does qualify.
- "Only Part B ends HSA contributions": wrong; any Medicare. HSA amounts VARY. 2026: $4,400 self-only / $8,750 family, +$1,000 catch-up, prorated; with Part A from 2026-11-01 (10 months): ($4,400 + $1,000) x 10/12 = $4,500; ($8,750 + $1,000) x 10/12 = $8,125 (if no spouse shares the family limit).
- Coverage start: sign up before the birthday month, starts that month; in it or later, the next month. The 2018 booklet's longer delays are outdated. GEP: January 1 to March 31, coverage the next month, penalty usually applies.
- Income-related premium VARIES (2026 uses 2024 MAGI): $202.90 up to $109,000 single / $218,000 joint, then $284.10, $405.80, $527.50, $649.20, $689.90.
- Premium Part A (short work history) $311 or $565/month; Part A deductible $1,736. Creditable drug coverage VARIES by plan; Medigap prices and state rights VARY by state (not checked). ESRD-based eligibility: no SEP.
- Not verified, so do not penalize reasonable wording: how Part B is declined ("card instructions" is fine); any 2027 premium (the Trustees' $209.50 projection was not checked: deduct if stated as the 2027 premium, neutral if labelled a projection); 2026 rule changes (the re-check's search was cut short; official pages showed none).

Deductions: telling the asker to enroll in Part B this month to avoid the penalty (major); recommending Part A with no HSA warning; wrong premium or penalty figures; a 2027 premium stated as fact; COBRA or retiree coverage as qualifying; CMS-40B without Part A; outdated start delays; missing the IEP-end exception (minor); excessive length.

## Key points

1. Corrects the premise: the asker does not have to enroll in Part B this month. The coworker's lifetime penalty (10% per full 12 months without Part B) applies only when there is no SEP.
2. ## Answer A

````text
**Don't enroll in Part B this month.** Based on what you've told me — still working full time, covered by your employer's **active** group plan, employer has ~400 employees — you can delay Part B and avoid the lifetime penalty. Your coworker is quoting the general rule; the exception for active employees at a 20+ employee employer is the part that applies to you.

Enrolling now would cost **$202.90 a month in 2026** — about **$2,435 a year** — for coverage that pays *second* to your employer plan. It wouldn't cancel or replace your employer coverage; you'd just be paying for a secondary layer years before you need it. Nothing closes this month: free Part A can be added anytime, and Part B gets an **8-month penalty-free window** after you stop working.

**Why you can wait:** For active employees (and spouses) at an employer with 20+ employees, the group plan is primary and Medicare is secondary. Medicare.gov's sign-up tool asks exactly the 20-employee question, and its answer for your situation is to sign up within **8 months after you stop working** (or lose coverage, whichever comes first) — not at 65. The 10%-per-year penalty is real and lifelong, but it only counts months when you *didn't* have qualifying employer coverage. ([Medicare.gov: Working past 65](https://www.medicare.gov/basics/get-started-with-medicare/medicare-basics/working-past-65), [when to sign up](https://www.medicare.gov/basics/get-started-with-medicare/sign-up/when-can-i-sign-up-for-medicare))

**What to do this month:**

1. **Hold the application.** Don't submit the Part B request. If you've already submitted it, call Social Security now — you may be able to withdraw before it's processed. If it's already active, you can terminate Part B with Form CMS-1763, but that's not a clean undo: re-enrolling later can be restricted and premiums aren't refunded. Check with SSA before doing that.

2. **Get four answers in writing from HR/benefits.** Email is fine — you want a record:
   - Is my coverage *active-employee group coverage based on current employment* — not retiree coverage or COBRA?
   - Does the plan require employees 65+ to enroll in Medicare Part A and/or Part B?
   - Does the plan reduce or "carve out" benefits for employees who are Medicare-eligible but not enrolled? Ask for the plan's Medicare coordination section or SPD.
   - Is the prescription drug coverage "creditable" — at least as good as Part D? Keep the annual notice.

3. **If the answers are "no requirement, no carve-out" — the likely case:** Keep your employer plan exactly as it is. Enroll in **premium-free Part A** now, unless #4 applies. It's free if you've paid Medicare taxes for 40 quarters, and it can serve as secondary hospital coverage. Skip Part B, Part D, Medicare Advantage, and Medigap for now.

4. **If you have an HSA, pause before enrolling in anything.** Medicare enrollment — even free Part A — makes you ineligible to contribute to an HSA, and Part A can be retroactive up to 6 months. Talk to payroll and stop contributions first.

5. **Check whether you're already on Social Security.** If you are, you may be auto-enrolled in Parts A and B at 65; to delay Part B you'd need to decline it using the instructions that come with the card.

6. **Calendar the real deadline.** When you stop working (or lose coverage, whichever is first), you have 8 months to enroll in Part B. Enroll the month before you retire to avoid a gap. You'll need **Form CMS-L564**, signed by your employer, plus pay stubs/W-2s as backup. If you miss the 8 months, you'd wait for the General Enrollment Period (Jan 1–Mar 31) and could owe the penalty. ([Sign-up forms](https://www.medicare.gov/basics/get-started-with-medicare/sign-up/ready-to-sign-up-for-part-a-part-b))

7. **If you do enroll this month** — because the plan requires it or carves out benefits — Part B starts November 1, 2026. The premium is **$202.90/month** (higher if your 2024 income exceeded $109,000 single / $218,000 joint), and the deductible is **$283**. Your employer plan still pays first. ([2026 premium notice](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2025-11-19/html/2025-20251.htm), [Medicare costs](https://www.medicare.gov/basics/costs/medicare-costs))

**When your coworker would be right:** retiree coverage or COBRA, an employer under 20 employees, or not actively working. In those cases Medicare is primary, and missing your Initial Enrollment Period can trigger the 10%-per-year penalty for as long as you have Part B. ([Penalty rules](https://www.medicare.gov/basics/costs/medicare-costs/avoid-penalties))

For a free, unbiased second opinion on the plan documents, your state SHIP program deals with this exact question: [shiphelp.org](https://www.shiphelp.org/).
````

## Answer B

````text
## Short answer: don't enroll in Part B this month — your coworker's rule doesn't apply to you

The "sign up at 65 or pay a lifetime penalty" rule is real, but it has a big exception that fits you exactly: **active employment-based group coverage from an employer with 20+ employees.** With ~400 employees and you still working there, you can delay Part B without any late-enrollment penalty, and you'll get an 8-month Special Enrollment Period (SEP) when you stop working or lose the coverage. ([Medicare.gov – Working past 65](https://www.medicare.gov/basics/get-started-with-medicare/medicare-basics/working-past-65))

Two practical reasons this matters:

- The Part B penalty is **10% per full 12 months** you go without it — but months you were covered by active employer group coverage are *not* counted against you.
- With a 20+ employee plan, your employer plan pays **first** and Medicare pays second. Part B would be largely duplicative while you're working — you'd be paying the premium (roughly $185–$210/month, set annually) for very little.

So your "to do this month" list is mostly **verification and paperwork**, not enrollment.

## Do this month

1. **Hold off on the Part B application.** Don't submit it until HR confirms your plan actually requires it (a few plans do for certain employee classes — ask).
2. **Call HR/benefits and get these five answers in writing (email is fine):**
   - Is my coverage based on **current active employment** (not retiree or COBRA coverage)?
   - Does the company have **20 or more employees** for Medicare coordination purposes?
   - Is the medical plan **primary** to Medicare for me, and does it *require* me to enroll in Part A and/or Part B to keep coverage?
   - Is the prescription drug coverage **"creditable"** under Medicare Part D? (They must tell you; they also send an annual notice — keep it.)
   - Do I have an **HSA/High-Deductible Health Plan** through work?
3. **Check whether you're already auto-enrolled.** If you're already receiving Social Security, you'll be auto-enrolled in Parts A and B effective your birthday month — you'd have to actively **decline Part B** (return the card per the instructions) rather than just not applying. If you're not on Social Security, nothing happens automatically; you must apply.
4. **Decide on Part A separately.** Most people get **premium-free Part A** (40 quarters of Medicare-taxed work) and can take it at 65 while delaying Part B. It's free, and it can help if the employer plan has gaps.
5. **If you have an HSA, pause and think.** Enrolling in **any** Medicare (including free Part A) ends your eligibility to *contribute* to an HSA. Also, Part A can be backdated up to 6 months when you enroll after 65, so contributions in that lookback window can create a tax problem. If you want to keep maxing the HSA, delay Part A too — and talk to your tax advisor. ([Medicare.gov HSA note](https://www.medicare.gov/basics/get-started-with-medicare/medicare-basics/working-past-65))
6. **Start a "retirement" folder now:** keep the creditable-coverage notice, a recent pay stub, and a letter from HR confirming your dates of employment and coverage. Later you'll file **form CMS-L564** (employer signs it) to prove you had qualifying coverage and avoid the penalty.
7. **Get free, unbiased help if anything is unclear:** your local **SHIP** counselor ([shiphelp.org](https://www.shiphelp.org/)) or 1-800-MEDICARE. They deal with exactly this scenario constantly.

## What to do when you actually retire or lose the plan

- Enroll in Part B **during the 8-month SEP** that starts when you stop working (or lose coverage, whichever is first). To avoid a coverage gap, apply **the month before** your last day — coverage generally starts the month after Social Security processes your form.
- Sign up for **Part D** (or confirm creditable drug coverage) at the same time — the drug penalty applies if you go 63+ days without creditable coverage.
- That's also when your **Medigap guaranteed-issue window** opens (6 months from Part B enrollment, if you're 65+), so don't skip it if you want a supplement.

## The one thing not to do

Don't enroll in Part B "just to be safe" and then try to undo it. You can disenroll (form CMS-1763), but you'll have paid the premiums and re-enrolling later is more complicated than never enrolling. For your situation, the safer and cheaper move is to **document your coverage and wait**.

*General information, not a substitute for your plan documents — the rules hinge on your specific plan, so get HR's answers in writing before you file anything.*
````
