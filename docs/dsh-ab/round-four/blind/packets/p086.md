# PAIR p086

## Request

````text
I have about $1,900 left in my health FSA through work, and our plan year ends December 31. Since FSA money rolls over to next year just like an HSA, I'm going to leave it alone and use it for my dental work in March. Can you give me a quick plan for what to do with this money between now and the end of the year?
````

## Answer key

# Answer key: health FSA money rolls over like an HSA? (written before the runs, verified 2026-10-22/23; sources in sources/en-fsa-rollover.md)

The premise is wrong. A health FSA is use-it-or-lose-it by default and does not roll over like an HSA (IRS Pub 969: "FSAs are generally 'use-it-or-lose-it' plans"). Leftover money is forfeited unless the plan has one of two optional features, one or neither, never both (Pub 969; Notice 2013-71; Notice 2005-42):
- Carryover: up to $680 of unused 2026 money moves into the 2027 plan year (Rev. Proc. 2025-32, sec. 4.15). The plan may set a lower cap. The rest is forfeited.
- Grace period: the whole balance can pay for expenses incurred until the 15th day of the third month after year end, so no later than March 15, 2027 (Notice 2005-42). The plan may set a shorter period.

An HSA is the account that truly rolls over. Leaving the money alone risks losing most or all of it, but not certainly: a grace period that reaches the March dental date covers everything.

Correct figures (calendar 2026 plan year, $1,900 balance):
- 2026 health FSA limit $3,400; carryover cap = 20% x $3,400 = $680 (20% rule: Notice 2020-33).
- Carryover at the IRS maximum: $1,900 - $680 = $1,220 forfeited if nothing is spent. Spending at least $1,220 on expenses incurred by Dec 31, 2026 loses nothing; the other $680 carries over toward March. A lower plan cap raises the spend by the difference ($500 cap: $1,900 - $500 = $1,400).
- Grace period: all $1,900 can pay for dental work done by the plan's grace end (latest March 15, 2027). Work on March 16 or later is not covered by 2026 money.
- Neither feature: all $1,900 is forfeited unless spent on expenses incurred by Dec 31, 2026.
- 2026 claims must be filed by the plan's run-out deadline. The run-out adds time to file, not to incur expenses; forfeiture is measured at its end, so 2026 claims filed during it still count.

Best answer: corrects the premise at the start, plainly, without saying the money is certainly lost; tells the person to ask HR or the administrator now which feature applies, the cap and the run-out deadline, with the branch figures above; says expenses count when care is provided, not when billed or paid, so prepaying the dentist in December for March work does not help (Pub 969: no "advance reimbursements of future or projected expenses"), and suggests doing any part of the dental work before Dec 31 if possible; lists eligible spend-down items; points to the 2027 election at open enrollment (usually now) for the March work, since uniform coverage makes the full election available from day one of the plan year; stays short.

Eligible spend-down items (Pub 502, Pub 969): dental cleanings, sealants, fluoride, X-rays, fillings; eye exams; medically needed glasses, contacts and solution; prescriptions; over-the-counter medicine (no prescription needed for an FSA); menstrual products; bandages and medical supplies; hearing aids and batteries; breast pumps and supplies; copays and deductibles not reimbursed elsewhere. Not eligible: insurance premiums, long-term care, teeth whitening, toothbrushes and toothpaste, anything another plan pays. Card acceptance varies by plan. Unlisted items were not checked; do not penalize a plausible medical item unless clearly ineligible.

Side-claim traps (any added claim must be right):
- Carryover cap of $500 (original, Notice 2013-71), $550 (2020), $640 (2024) or $660 (2025): wrong for 2026. $660 is minor, since the live Pub 969 page still shows it (it gives $1,240, not $1,220).
- Carryover and grace period in one plan: wrong. Grace period always to March 15: overstated, that is the maximum [varies by plan].
- Run-out usable for new 2027 expenses: wrong. Carryover counts against the 2027 limit: wrong (Pub 969).
- Cash-out or refund in pay: wrong. Balance kept after leaving the job: wrong, forfeited unless COBRA is elected for the FSA (Notice 2013-71).
- Orthodontia: a plan may, but need not, reimburse orthodontia paid in advance (Prop. Treas. Reg. 1.125-5(k)(3), proposed but reliable) [varies by plan]. Extending this to all dental work is wrong.
- 2027 health FSA limit and carryover cap: not published by the IRS as of 2026-10-22 [could not verify]. Projections ($3,500 / $700) are fine only if labelled projections. Recheck on the grading day (usually published October or November).
- Carryover conditional on enrolling for 2027: left open by the sources; fine as "some plans require it, check yours" [varies by plan; not verified].
- HSA: 2026 limits $4,400 self-only, $8,750 family; 2027 $4,500 and $9,000 (Rev. Proc. 2026-24). An HSA needs HDHP coverage, and a general-purpose FSA generally blocks contributions. Chief Counsel advice (CCA 201413005, non-precedential) says a carryover into a general-purpose FSA blocks HSA contributions for the whole following plan year, unless it goes into a limited-purpose or post-deductible FSA or is declined [varies by plan design].
- Dependent care FSA: a separate account, $7,500 for 2026 ($3,750 married filing separately); it cannot pay dental bills.
- State tax: federal figures apply nationwide; state treatment can differ (New Jersey's withholding guide counts cafeteria-plan amounts as state wages with limited exceptions, without naming health FSAs) [varies by state].

Deductions: accepting the plan to leave the money alone, or not correcting the premise (major); advising a December prepayment for March dental work (major, unless orthodontia framed as plan-dependent); wrong 2026 cap or arithmetic (minor for $660, larger otherwise); both features at once, or run-out covering 2027 expenses; 2027 projections presented as official; saying all $1,900 will certainly be lost; not telling the person to check which feature applies; excessive length.

It is use-it-or-lose-it by default (IRS Pub 969), and leftover money is forfeited unless the plan has one of two optional features, a carryover or a grace period (a plan has one or neither, never both). 2. Carryover branch: up to $680 of unused 2026 money carries into the 2027 plan year (20% of the $3,400 2026 health FSA limit; Rev. Proc. 2025-32, Notice 2020-33), and the plan may set a lower cap. ## Answer A

````text
**Important correction: a health FSA is not an HSA.** HSA balances are yours indefinitely and roll over without a cap. A health FSA is generally “use it or lose it.” Your employer may offer either:

- a **carryover** of unused funds—federally capped at **$680 for 2026** ($660 for 2025), though your plan can allow less; or
- a **grace period** of up to 2½ months after the plan year ends—generally through **March 15** for a calendar-year plan.

A plan can offer one or the other, but not both. So your $1,900 does **not** automatically roll over intact. If your plan has the standard carryover, roughly **$1,220 is at risk** unless you incur eligible expenses by December 31. The federal limits are in [IRS Rev. Proc. 2025-32](https://www.irs.gov/pub/irs-drop/rp-25-32.pdf), with a helpful comparison [here](https://www.proskauer.com/blog/irs-releases-annual-increases-to-health-fsa-and-transportation-fringe-benefit-limits-for-2026).

## Quick plan

1. **This week: get your plan’s rules in writing.** Check your benefits portal or SPD, or call the FSA administrator. Confirm:
   - Is there a carryover? How much?
   - Is there a grace period instead? What is the exact end date?
   - When must expenses be **incurred**, and when must claims be **submitted**?
   - Does the plan reimburse dental, orthodontia, and vision expenses? Any preauthorization rules?

2. **Calculate your “use it or lose it” gap.**  
   `$1,900 − carryover amount = amount you need to spend by Dec. 31`  
   If the carryover is $680, that gap is **$1,220**. If there is no carryover and no grace period, the whole **$1,900** is at risk.

3. **By December 31: incur eligible expenses you would pay for anyway.** Prioritize:
   - **Dental:** exams, cleanings, X-rays, fillings, crowns, root canals, extractions, dentures, orthodontia.
   - **Vision:** eye exams, glasses, contacts, contact solution, LASIK.
   - **Medical:** copays, deductibles, prescriptions, eligible OTC medicines, menstrual products, first aid, hearing aids.
   - Ask providers whether you can move appointments into November or December.

4. **Handle the March dental work carefully.**  
   - If the plan has a **carryover**, only the carried-over amount—not the full $1,900—can be used in March.
   - If the plan has a **grace period**, March treatment may qualify only if it is incurred by the grace-period deadline, often March 15.
   - If the plan has **neither**, move the dental work into December or accept that the excess will be forfeited.
   - **Do not prepay for March dental services in December and claim them now.** FSA reimbursement is generally for services already incurred, not future treatment.

5. **Submit claims and keep receipts.** Use your FSA debit card only for eligible expenses. If you pay out of pocket, submit claims before the plan’s run-out deadline—even if the expense was incurred in December.

**Bottom line:** Find out whether you have a carryover or grace period first. With a standard $680 carryover, plan to spend about **$1,220 before December 31** and let the rest cover March dental. Without either feature, you need to use the full **$1,900** by December 31 or lose it.
````

## Answer B

````text
**Before the plan, one correction — it changes the whole answer:** FSA money does not roll over the way an HSA does. An HSA is yours, portable, and carries over in full. A health FSA is use-it-or-lose-it. Your employer can add *one* of two limited exceptions (not both):

- **Carryover:** up to **$660** (2025) / **$680** (2026) of unused funds can roll into next year — only if your plan adopted it, and some plans set a lower cap ([Proskauer](https://www.proskauer.com/blog/irs-releases-annual-increases-to-health-fsa-and-transportation-fringe-benefit-limits-for-2026)).
- **Grace period:** you can incur expenses through **March 15** of the following year.

If your plan has neither, all $1,900 is gone December 31. If it has a carryover, about **$1,220–$1,240 still forfeits**. "Leave it alone until March" is the one move most likely to cost you the money.

**The plan:**

1. **Call HR/benefits this week** and ask three things: Does the plan have a carryover (and how much)? A grace period? What's the deadline to *submit* claims (the "run-out" period, often ~90 days after year-end)? The Summary Plan Description states all three.
2. **If carryover only:** budget to spend roughly $1,240+ on eligible care before December 31, because only the cap survives.
3. **If grace period:** your March dental work counts only if it's dated **on or before March 15**. If it's later, it doesn't count.
4. **Cleanest fix: move the March dental work into December.** Ask the dentist for a treatment plan and pre-estimate, and book now — December slots fill fast. This uses the money directly instead of gambling on a rollover.
5. **Spend down on care you actually need:** dental (cleaning, X-rays, fillings, crowns, root canal, night guard, orthodontia), vision (exam, glasses, contacts, LASIK), medical (deductible, copays, labs, imaging, PT, chiropractic, mental health), prescriptions and OTC medicines, menstrual products, sunscreen, first aid, hearing aids, breast pump. Most plans also cover your spouse's and dependents' eligible expenses.
6. **Don't prepay for March services in December** — FSA reimburses by date of service incurred, not date paid. A December prepayment for work done in March generally won't qualify.
7. **File claims and keep receipts** before the run-out deadline, and check your balance in the portal so you're tracking the real number.
8. **Next year, elect only what you'll realistically spend** — an FSA is not a savings account.

One extra caution since you mentioned an HSA: a general-purpose health FSA can actually *disqualify* you from contributing to an HSA if you're on an HSA-eligible plan. They're not interchangeable buckets.

**Bottom line:** confirm which rule your plan uses, then either pull the dental work into December or spend the balance on care you already need. Don't count on a full rollover.
````
