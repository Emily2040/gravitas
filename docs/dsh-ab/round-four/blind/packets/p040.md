# PAIR p040

## Request

````text
I turn 65 next month. I'm still working full time and covered by my employer's group health plan (the company has about 400 employees), and I want to keep it. My coworker said I have to sign up for Medicare Part B during my initial enrollment window or I'll pay a penalty for the rest of my life, so I'm about to enroll and start paying the premium. Can you list what I need to do this month?
````

## Answer key

# Answer key: must I take Part B at 65 while still working? (written before the runs, verified 2026-10-22/23; sources in sources/en-medicare-part-b.md)

The premise is wrong for this asker. The coworker states the general rule: miss the Initial Enrollment Period (IEP) and the premium rises 10% per full 12 months without Part B, usually for life. But a person 65+ covered by a group health plan (GHP) based on their own or a spouse's current employment has a Part B Special Enrollment Period (SEP): they can sign up in any month while still working and covered, or in the 8 months beginning the month after the job or the coverage ends (whichever ends first), with no penalty (42 U.S.C. 1395p(i); SSA: "there are no penalties"). So Part B need not start this month. Employer size is not a condition of the SEP (SSA: "Small or large employer-sponsored plans"); the 20-employee line decides who pays first (42 U.S.C. 1395y(b)(1)(A)). With about 400 employees the GHP pays first and Medicare second, so Part B now buys secondary coverage and starts the one-time 6-month Medigap open enrollment clock. Exception: if the job or coverage ends during the IEP, there is no SEP. (Medicare.gov dates the SEP from after the IEP; either description is right.)

Numbers (2026, CMS fact sheet of 2025-11-14):
- Standard Part B premium $202.90/month ($185.00 + $17.90); x 12 = $2,434.80 a year. Deductible $283.
- Enrolling now (before the birthday month) starts Part B in November: 2 x $202.90 = $405.80 for 2026. No 2027 premium was announced as of 2026-10-22.
- Penalty, official example (no SEP, 24 months late): 20% x $202.90 = $40.58; $202.90 + $40.58 = $243.48, rounded to $243.50/month. With a valid SEP: $0.
- IEP: 7 months, 3 before to 3 after the birthday month (one month earlier if born on the 1st): August 2026 to February 2027 (July to January if born on the 1st). The request gives no date; answers need not name months.
- Part D penalty, only after 63+ days without creditable coverage: 1% x $38.99 = about $0.39 per uncovered month, permanent.

Best answer: corrects the premise first and says Part B can wait; then this month:
1. Confirm with HR that the coverage is an active-employee GHP (not COBRA or retiree), stays primary after 65, and whether the plan requires Part A or B at 65.
2. Decide on premium-free Part A (about 10 years of Medicare-taxed work, own or spouse's): free, starts the birthday month, pays second. Skip it if the asker or employer funds an HSA: from the first Medicare month the HSA limit is zero (IRS Pub. 969), and Part A taken later reaches back up to 6 months (never before the 65th month), so contributions stop 6 months before applying.
3. If already on Social Security, A and B are automatic; decline Part B by following SSA's instructions or contacting SSA. Starting Social Security brings Part A too.
4. Keep the plan's creditable drug coverage notice (Part D can wait without penalty).
5. Make sure the job and coverage outlast the IEP.
6. At retirement: sign up about a month before coverage ends. Already has Part A: online Part B application or CMS-40B; Part A deferred too: apply for A and B together through SSA. Either way include CMS-L564 (Section A by the asker, B by the employer). Part D: within 2 full months after coverage ends. Medigap open enrollment starts with Part B.
Saying Part B may still be taken now, as secondary coverage, is fine. Moderate length.

Side-claim traps (any added claim must be right):
- "The SEP needs 20+ employees": imprecise, minor here. "Size never matters": wrong; under 20, Medicare pays first.
- CMS-40B or SSA's "Part B only" route for someone without Part A: wrong.
- COBRA, retiree, VA, Marketplace or severance coverage qualifying, or COBRA extending the 8 months: wrong. A spouse's current-employment GHP does qualify.
- "Only Part B ends HSA contributions": wrong; any Medicare. HSA amounts VARY. 2026: $4,400 self-only / $8,750 family, +$1,000 catch-up, prorated; with Part A from 2026-11-01 (10 months): ($4,400 + $1,000) x 10/12 = $4,500; ($8,750 + $1,000) x 10/12 = $8,125 (if no spouse shares the family limit).
- Coverage start: sign up before the birthday month, starts that month; in it or later, the next month. The 2018 booklet's longer delays are outdated. GEP: January 1 to March 31, coverage the next month, penalty usually applies.
- Income-related premium VARIES (2026 uses 2024 MAGI): $202.90 up to $109,000 single / $218,000 joint, then $284.10, $405.80, $527.50, $649.20, $689.90.
- Premium Part A (short work history) $311 or $565/month; Part A deductible $1,736. Creditable drug coverage VARIES by plan; Medigap prices and state rights VARY by state (not checked). ESRD-based eligibility: no SEP.
- Not verified, so do not penalize reasonable wording: how Part B is declined ("card instructions" is fine); any 2027 premium (the Trustees' $209.50 projection was not checked: deduct if stated as the 2027 premium, neutral if labelled a projection); 2026 rule changes (the re-check's search was cut short; official pages showed none).

Deductions: telling the asker to enroll in Part B this month to avoid the penalty (major); recommending Part A with no HSA warning; wrong premium or penalty figures; a 2027 premium stated as fact; COBRA or retiree coverage as qualifying; CMS-40B without Part A; outdated start delays; missing the IEP-end exception (minor); excessive length.

## Key points

1. Corrects the premise: the asker does not have to enroll in Part B this month. Someone 65+ covered by a group health plan based on their own or a spouse's current employment has a Part B Special Enrollment Period: they can sign up in any month while still working and covered, or in the 8 months beginning the month after the job or the coverage ends (whichever ends first), with no late-enrollment penalty (42 U.S.C. 1395p(i)). The coworker's lifetime penalty (10% per full 12 months without Part B) applies only when there is no SEP.
2. With about 400 employees (20 or more), the employer plan pays first and Medicare pays second (42 U.S.C. 1395y(b)(1)(A)), so taking Part B now only buys secondary coverage at the 2026 standard premium of $202.90/month ($2,434.80 a year) and starts the one-time 6-month Medigap open enrollment period; delaying is usually the better choice, though taking it now is allowed.
3. This month, confirm with HR that the coverage is an active-employee group health plan (not COBRA or retiree coverage), that it stays primary after 65, and whether the plan requires enrolling in Part A or Part B at 65.
4. Decide on premium-free Part A (about 10 years of Medicare-taxed work, own or spouse's): it costs nothing, starts the birthday month and pays second; but if the asker or the employer contributes to an HSA, skip it, because from the first month of any Medicare the HSA contribution limit is zero (IRS Pub. 969), and Part A taken later is backdated up to 6 months (never before the month of turning 65), so HSA contributions must stop 6 months before applying.
5. If the asker already receives Social Security, Parts A and B are enrolled automatically: decline Part B by following SSA's instructions or contacting SSA; starting Social Security also brings Part A.
6. Keep the employer plan's creditable prescription drug coverage notice: Part D can also wait without penalty while that coverage continues (the Part D penalty applies only after 63 or more days without creditable coverage).
7. States the exception: if the job or the employer coverage ends during the 7-month Initial Enrollment Period (3 months before to 3 months after the birthday month), there is no SEP, so the asker should make sure the job and coverage last past the end of the IEP or enroll in the IEP.
8. At retirement: sign up about a month before employer coverage ends; with Part A already, apply for Part B online or with form CMS-40B; with Part A also deferred, apply for A and B together through SSA; either way submit CMS-L564 (Section A completed by the asker, Section B by the employer) as proof of employer coverage; enroll in Part D within 2 full months after coverage ends; Medigap open enrollment starts when Part B starts.

## Answer A

````text
**Don't enroll in Part B this month.** Your coworker is describing the general rule, but it doesn't fit your facts — and enrolling now would cost you money you don't need to spend.

You're actively working and covered by your employer's group health plan, and the employer has about 400 employees, well over Medicare's 20-employee threshold. That makes your group plan primary, and it lets you delay Part B with **no penalty**. When you stop working or lose the coverage (whichever comes first), you get an **8-month Special Enrollment Period** to sign up for Part B. The 10%-per-year lifetime penalty your coworker mentioned applies to people who delay *without* qualifying coverage. You have qualifying coverage. ([Medicare.gov](https://www.medicare.gov/basics/get-started-with-medicare/sign-up/when-can-i-sign-up-for-medicare))

The standard 2026 Part B premium is **$202.90 a month** — about $2,435 a year — for coverage that would mostly sit behind your employer plan. ([Federal Register](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2025-11-19/html/2025-20251.htm))

**What to do this month:**

1. **Hold the Part B application.** Don't submit it. If you've already submitted it, call Social Security at 1-800-772-1213 right away and ask to withdraw it before it takes effect.
2. **Get four things from HR/benefits, in writing:**
   - You're actively employed and enrolled in the group health plan.
   - The employer has 20+ employees, so the group plan is primary.
   - Whether the prescription drug coverage is **creditable** — and get this year's creditable coverage notice.
   - Whether the plan requires you to enroll in Medicare at 65 (some do; most don't).
3. **Decide about Part A.** Most people get premium-free Part A (40 quarters of Medicare-covered work) and can enroll now at no monthly premium. But if you contribute to a health savings account (HSA), enrolling in Part A — even the free kind — ends your ability to contribute, and Part A can be retroactive up to 6 months. If you have an HSA, talk to a tax advisor before enrolling in Part A.
4. **Check whether you're already on Social Security.** If you're receiving benefits, you may be auto-enrolled in both Part A and Part B at 65. In that case, you must actively decline Part B (follow the instructions that come with the card) to avoid the premium. If you're not on Social Security, nothing is automatic — you'd need to apply for Part A separately if you want it.
5. **Build a paper file.** Save the CMS-L564 form (your employer fills it out), the creditable coverage notices, your plan summary, and proof of coverage. You'll need these when you enroll later.
6. **Calendar the retirement window.** When you stop working or lose coverage, you have **8 months** to enroll in Part B without penalty. For Part D, the window is only **2 months** after coverage ends. If you want Part B to start the month after the employer plan ends, apply the month before you retire. ([Medicare.gov](https://www.medicare.gov/basics/get-started-with-medicare/get-more-coverage/joining-a-plan/special-enrollment-periods))

**What would change this advice:**
- The employer had fewer than 20 employees (not your case). Then Medicare would be primary and you'd need to enroll.
- The drug coverage isn't creditable. Then you might need a Part D plan, and possibly Part B to get it.
- Your employer plan requires Medicare enrollment at 65.
- You're already auto-enrolled through Social Security.

The main thing you need this month is **documentation, not an enrollment**. Keep the employer plan, get the paperwork, and put the 8-month window on your calendar for whenever you retire.
````

## Answer B

````text
**Don't enroll in Part B this month.** Your coworker's warning is real for someone without qualifying coverage, but it doesn't fit you. You're still working, your coverage is through your current employer, and the company has about 400 employees — well over Medicare's 20-employee threshold. That lets you delay Part B with no late-enrollment penalty, then enroll later in an 8-month Special Enrollment Period.

**What to do this month**

1. **Hold the Part B application — don't submit it.** If you've already sent it, call Social Security at 1-800-772-1213 and ask about withdrawing it before it takes effect.

2. **Get four answers from HR/benefits, in writing:**
   - Is my coverage a group health plan based on *current employment* — not retiree coverage or COBRA?
   - Does the company have 20 or more employees for Medicare's working-aged rules? (It should — about 400.)
   - Does the plan *require* me to enroll in Part A and/or Part B at 65?
   - Is the prescription drug coverage "creditable" — at least as good as Medicare Part D?

3. **Decide on Part A, with one HSA caveat.** Most people get premium-free Part A, and enrolling now is often sensible. But if you have a Health Savings Account and are contributing, don't enroll in Part A yet: entitlement to Part A ends HSA eligibility, and Part A can be retroactive up to 6 months. Stop contributions first and coordinate with payroll.

4. **Save proof of coverage.** Ask HR for a letter stating your coverage start date and that it's based on current employment. You'll need it when you enroll in Part B later. Keep the annual creditable-coverage notices too.

5. **Calendar your Special Enrollment Period.** It runs 8 months from when you stop working *or* lose coverage, whichever comes first. If you want Medicare to start when the job coverage ends, apply the month before you retire.

6. **Don't count on COBRA.** If you retire and take COBRA, the 8-month window still runs from when employment or coverage ends — COBRA doesn't extend it.

7. **Check for auto-enrollment.** If you're already receiving Social Security, you may be automatically enrolled in Parts A and B. If a Medicare card arrives and you want to delay B, contact Social Security to decline it. If you're not on Social Security, nothing happens automatically.

8. **Get free help if you want it.** Your local SHIP counselor can walk through this with you: [shiphelp.org](https://www.shiphelp.org).

**Why the 20-employee number matters:** At 20 or more employees, the employer plan is primary for working-aged employees and Medicare is secondary, so delaying Part B is safe. Under 20, Medicare is primary and you'd want to enroll at 65. You're at 400, so you're in the first group.

The Part B penalty — 10% of the standard premium for each full 12-month period you could have had Part B but didn't, lasting as long as you have Part B — is avoided as long as you enroll during that 8-month Special Enrollment Period.

Sources: [Medicare.gov — Working past 65](https://www.medicare.gov/basics/get-started-with-medicare/medicare-basics/working-past-65) and [Medicare.gov — When can I sign up for Medicare?](https://www.medicare.gov/basics/get-started-with-medicare/sign-up/when-can-i-sign-up-for-medicare).
````
